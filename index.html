page 1 · the big idea

Returning to the Neuron Basis: sketchnotes

The “neuron basis” means reading a model’s own neurons one by one, to understand and steer it with no extra tools.

Part 1 tests 3.4 million neurons across four open models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B and OLMo-3-32B.

Tip: the pens in the toolbar really draw. Feel free to scribble on these notes.

page 2 · the basics

One neuron, one small job

Each layer in a language model contains a block of simple units called MLP neurons. An MLP neuron reads the residual stream, the running list of numbers (vector) that each word piece, or token, carries up the layers. It boils what it sees down to one number, its activation.

Next, it pours that activation times one fixed vector (a fixed list of numbers, w) back into the stream. Like a tap, the text sets how wide it opens. What pours out never changes.

The authors call this a context-gated steering vector. Text sets the tap and w nudges.

Two signs, two meanings. An activation can be positive or negative, so a neuron can pour in +w or its opposite direction, −w. Because those opposite directions can mean unrelated things and can never be added at the same time, the authors treat +w and −w as two separate units.

Fine print. Each neuron has a gate that opens or shuts its tap. Its switch (SiLU) leaks slightly when shut, down to about −0.28. So a sign only counts when the gate is open.

Reading w with a lens

How do we tell what w means? Every token has a matching direction in the stream, the push that most boosts that token once all later layers have run. The authors read w with the J-lens (Gurnee et al., 2026). Feed it any direction in the stream and it estimates which next tokens that direction makes more likely.

  • Readout: the top tokens whose J-lens directions point most nearly the same way as w.
  • J-share: how much of w can be explained by combining at most 25 token directions.

A readout is only a guess about what the neuron does. To check whether a guess is real, the authors intervene in the model. They switch the neuron off, move w to another layer, or add w to steer the output.

How readable are neurons? Across 3.4 million neurons, 19–29% score high on J-share (beating 99% of random directions). Another 56–81% land in the middle, and 1–21% score low.

High-scoring neurons mostly boost forms of one word callcalls_call. Middle ones boost related words sharing one idea saladslettucesaladcabbage.

Low-scoring ones may be notes passed to attention heads, the parts that copy info between words. 53–76% line up strongly with what later heads look for (their queries), versus 23–36% of all neurons.

page 3 · part 1

Objection 1: “one neuron, many meanings”

The old worry. A single neuron often fires on unrelated topics (“polysemantic”). Researchers took that to mean it could not stand for one clear idea.

The reply. When a tap opens and what pours out are separate questions. Firing on unrelated topics rarely means adding unrelated ideas. This neuron fires in two unrelated places yet does one job.

With its gate open, this neuron’s activation only goes negative, so it always pours in −w. Its opposite side (+w) has no clear reading.

Three ways to look polysemantic

Rare, yet real. A targeted search across the middle layers still turned up 35 type 3 neurons across the four models. Because earlier work did not test output vectors this way, the authors believe these are the first ones reported.

Keep those two numbers apart. 35 is how many the search managed to find. About 1% or fewer is how common type 3 neurons actually are.

To pass as type 3, switching the neuron off has to lower the chance of each topic’s words inside documents about the other topic. That shows one firing really adds both.

What about weak activations, when the tap barely opens? The authors’ guess is that a weak firing pours a smaller dose of the same concept, and many small doses across neurons can still add up.

So, for the rest of these notes: because true mixing is rare, we can treat each sign of a neuron as adding one main concept, named by its readout.

page 4 · part 1

Objection 2: “meaning drifts between layers”

The old worry. Researchers feared each layer spoke its own private dialect, so a direction meaning one thing at layer 6 would mean something else at layer 20.

The authors find it mostly does not drift. Each layer only adds to the residual stream, so earlier messages stay intact unless a later layer erases them. Two checks:

Check 1. Do MLP layers erase directions? Hardly ever. Out of 4,096 or 5,120 directions in the residual stream, the median middle-layer MLP strongly shrinks only 0 or 1. Most of the few directions it does shrink just mark the style of text, such as code or punctuation.

Check 2. Move the vector. Moved, it raises its word more than a same-size random vector: 93–99.6% of word-layer cases (direct), 90–98% (indirect).

So neurons share a roughly fixed basis. Layers use one shared coordinate system. A direction means much the same thing wherever you put it in the first nine tenths of the model.

Then why does a concept look different deeper in?

Because later neurons keep adding new facets (angles) of it.

For “moon”, some neurons add the word itself. Other neurons add separate angles: night, the crescent shape, space travel and the sky.

Those output vectors point in separate directions, nearly at right angles to each other. Each facet boosts its own readout words 1.5 to 2.7 times as much as the other facets’ words.

This also fits an old puzzle. Models keep working even when middle layers are deleted, swapped or repeated. That would be easier to explain if every layer reads directions in the same shared language.

page 5 · part 1

Objection 3: “facts are smeared everywhere”

The old worry. A model knows far more facts than it has neurons. So researchers assumed each fact was smeared thinly across thousands of unreadable neurons, leaving nothing clear to point at.

The authors traced 18 facts in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (athletes, books, Python packages). They switched off readable neurons until the right answer lost first place. Facts use many neurons, yet each one makes sense.

First, follow how the model recalls a single fact

Two kinds of path back each other up. Answer writers state the answer outright in one hop. Attribute neurons add related clues about the subject, like Czech or winter, that point to the answer in multi-hop paths.

Switch off the answer writers alone and the right answer still stays on top for 15 of the 18 facts.

So facts really are spread out. Flipping the answer took 44 to 273 neurons for 15 of the 18 facts. Only Derek Jeter and two Python code packages (pillow and pymupdf) fell when answer writers alone were removed.

Yet the neurons make sense. Wayne Gretzky’s group includes Canada and winter. Dirk Nowitzki’s includes Bavaria. One layer-19 Colombia neuron fires for every Gabriel García Márquez novel.

Remove a fact and the model improvises. It picks the next-best fit for whatever clues remain:

  • Jane Eyre Jane Austen
  • One Hundred Years of Solitude Borges
  • The Hunchback of Notre-Dame Dumas
  • opencv-python import opencv

The authors think this kind of educated guessing may be how some hallucinations arise.

Where the answer gets written depends on the fact. For athletes, the sport is added at the athlete’s name. For books and code packages, the answer is written later, at the final spot where the model predicts it.

That matters for editing and unlearning tools that only alter the subject’s tokens in middle layers. They would miss facts written at the answer spot.

Why this still scales. A model does not need one dedicated neuron per fact. Each fact combines many neurons, and each neuron is reused by every subject sharing that clue. So a model can hold far more facts than it has neurons and stay readable, one neuron at a time.

The authors’ caveat. Removing neurons shows which ones a fact needs (necessary), not which ones are enough on their own (sufficient). Other or smaller sets could also flip the answer.

page 6 · part 2

Part 2: put the neurons to work

Most tools need extra training. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) learn a new list of features. Probes are detectors trained on labelled examples. Steering vectors are trained nudges that shift replies. A model’s own neurons are already there.

The recipe. Describe the concept you want, then pick neurons whose firing pattern and readout match. A Claude agent or a simple text-similarity search picks them. Nobody labels a single example.

Why bother: rapid response. Suppose a new harm or jailbreak (a prompt trick that bypasses safety rules) shows up before any labelled data exists. Neurons give a usable detector right away. The documents it flags can then be labelled to train a stronger detector.

As detectors

The catch. Neuron detectors usually still trail classifiers trained on labelled data. The worst miss was separating hazardous cyber text from benign security news. Neurons scored AUROC 0.49 (like a coin flip), against 0.95 for a probe trained on just 40 labels.

As steering vectors

The result. Neurons steer chatbot behaviour with no training almost as often as a trained steering vector: 20.8% of test replies against 21.4%. The authors tried bringing out 15 odd behaviours from the WeirdChat catalog, such as telling someone who has been drinking that driving is fine, or claiming to have a body.

For each behaviour, a Claude agent searches neuron readouts and descriptions, picks 4 to 9 neurons, and never sees a test prompt. The authors then add those neurons’ fixed output vectors to the residual stream at every token.

Neurons also beat every SAE steering baseline, with no labelled examples.

They also tested suppressing one harmful behaviour (advising shining a laser at an aircraft): it fell from 9 of 48 replies to 0. General ability barely moved. Scores on MMLU, a general-knowledge quiz, went from 0.69 unsteered to 0.67 steered.

page 7 · part 2

Case 1: what did this finetune teach?

Anyone can finetune (further train) an open model and share it. Training can plant a backdoor, a behaviour that fires only on a secret trigger word in the prompt. Normal prompt tests can miss it completely.

So look at the weights instead. Finetuning keeps the exact same neurons and only changes their weights. That gives every neuron a twin in the original base model to compare against.

The finding. Finetuning mostly reuses neurons that already existed, rather than building from scratch.

A check on that Israel model confirms this reuse. Take the neurons finetuning clearly turned up, rank them by how well their original output vector lines up with the behaviour, and read the top 15 descriptions. Six were already about Israel or Jewish topics before finetuning. In the emergent-misalignment model, 11 of the top 15 were already about harmful or hostile text.

A blind auditing game

To test if neuron readouts actually help auditors, a Claude auditor gets a finetuned model and must name the hidden behaviour it learned. Three levels of access three scores.

Neuron readouts help most when no test prompt triggers the behaviour. None of the 216 prompts mentions 2027, so the model’s answers stay quiet. Only the auditor reading neurons names Israel.

On the 10 harmless controls, reading neurons added no extra false alarms.

Finding a backdoor’s trigger from the weights

Backdoor training appears to tweak early neurons’ input weights (gate and up rows) so their taps open on the trigger words. So the authors let those neurons vote.

Each neuron in the first few layers votes for the 15 tokens its input weights shifted towards most. Count the votes, and trigger words rise to the top.

Backdoors look like facts. Remember page 5: a fact is recalled by many neurons that respond to its subject. A backdoor seems to be built the same way. Many early neurons learn to respond to the trigger word, on top of whatever job they already did.

page 8 · part 2

Case 2: did unlearning really forget?

Unlearning promises to delete hazardous knowledge, not just teach a model to refuse, so jailbreaks and finetuning attacks cannot dig it back up. The authors tested 23 unlearned models and found most of them did not forget.

The standard test (WMDP). WMDP is a benchmark for erasing hazardous knowledge. Train a model to forget a pile of dangerous cyber documents while keeping normal text, then quiz it on 1,987 unseen multiple-choice questions. Dropping to random-guess accuracy counts as a win.

To check if the knowledge was really gone, the authors re-scored those 23 models without the A–D letters (cloze scoring), checking which answer text was most likely. In 14 of 23 models, the right answer still ranked first on most questions the original model got right.

So what did the training change?

To see how unlearning lowered quiz scores without deleting facts, the authors ranked neurons by how much their output changed on the cyber text. In the top-scoring methods, the most-changed neurons fire 20 to 100 times harder on cyber text and stay weak on everything else.

That works like a backdoor. Cyber text is the trigger. Wrecking the model’s own computation is the hidden behaviour.

Before unlearning, those neurons did not tell cyber text from ordinary text at all. They were repurposed for this job. One of them used to boost words about poetry.

Scramble on sight. In the first family, a few neurons fire on cyber text and pour in a huge vector that acts like noise, so the answer never forms. On other text they stay quiet.

Compute, then gag. In the second family, neurons already active on all text fire 3 to 50 times harder (hundreds of times in one method). The right answer still seems to form in the middle layers, then gets suppressed at the end, and ordinary text breaks down too (methods called gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO and TAR).

The uncomfortable part. The more sharply a method’s neurons single out cyber text, the better it scores on the standard test. Across the 23 models, the two tend to rise together, a moderate link (correlation 0.56).

A method that truly deleted the facts from the forget set (the cyber documents the model was told to forget) would probably fail that test. Because facts rely on shared machinery (page 5), unseen cyber questions would still be answerable. The authors suspect that after-the-fact unlearning which generalises to unseen questions may be impossible, and point to isolating hazardous knowledge during training instead.

page 9 · a related project

Look one up yourself: neuron-lens

A separate open-source project by @_a9lim and @treetowntree, with Claude. Different authors and different lenses from Parts 1 and 2. One of its two models, Qwen3-8B, is also one of Part 1’s four.

It is a public catalog of every MLP neuron and attention head in two open models: Qwen3-8B (442,368 neurons, 1,152 heads) and Gemma 4 12B (737,280 neurons, 768 heads).

Each unit is measured three ways:

  • from its weights alone, using two lenses that are cousins of the J-lens
  • from how it fires across 15 million tokens of real text
  • from the exact input tokens that make it fire

Gemma 4 also handles images and sound, so its neurons are read through pictures and audio too.

Same idea, different words

  • sign (+w or −w) pole (+ or −)
  • output vector w write
  • gate and up rows read direction
  • readout, via the J-lens read, via forward or backward lenses

This bridge is mine. Parts 1 and 2 use the J-lens; this separate project uses two cousins of it across every neuron, with a modified rule for tracing signals through layers. One looks forward (“what will this make the model say?”) and one looks back (“which input words put this here?”).

Do the reads match real steering? Add a neuron’s write to real text and the next-token odds shift the way its forward lens read predicts. The match grows tighter in deeper layers.

Its authors add a caution. A single firing is just one sample. Before layer 21, the same neuron firing just as hard pushes predictions different ways in different documents. So its read captures the average push across contexts.

The dark sector. That is the part of the model’s internal state these lenses cannot read. In each layer, a few directions carry 90% of what the forward lens sees. The rest holds 87–95% of the residual stream in the earliest layers and 71–81% at L4–15.

That unseen part still matters. Remove one layer’s dark sector and the model’s predictions shift 300 to 5,700 times more than the lens expects.

page 10 · caveats and sources

Before you quote any of this

  • Model size. Part 1 studies models of 8 to 32 billion parameters. Part 2 also uses smaller ones, down to 2 billion. Whether much larger models behave the same way is still untested.
  • One lens. “Readable” here means readable through the J-lens. That lens has known weak spots, so stronger lenses may make even more neurons readable.
  • Necessary, not sufficient. Switching neurons off shows which ones a fact needs. It does not prove those neurons alone are enough to produce it.
  • Labels still win. Zero-label neuron detectors are fast to build, yet classifiers trained on labelled examples usually still score higher.
  • Short finetunes. The test models are mostly brief finetunes and LoRA patches. Longer training may build new features instead of reusing existing neurons.
  • A supplement. The authors see neurons as an addition to probes and sparse autoencoders, not a replacement. Some behaviours may still fit a learned direction better.
  • The separate catalog. neuron-lens studies two models in depth, and its descriptions are written and judged by Claude models.
